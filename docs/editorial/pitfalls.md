# 3.15 Common Editorial Pitfalls

*Last reviewed by the SAP DTP team: 2026-05-13.*

A curated, evidence-based list of errors and patterns SAP editors **recurrently fix** in BAPS English manuscripts. This is **not** a general grammar guide — see [Garner's Modern English Usage](https://www.oup.com/), [Fowler's Modern English Usage](https://global.oup.com/academic/), or the [Chicago Manual of Style ch. 5](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/) for those questions. This page documents only the patterns specific to:

1. **Indian English** — usages well-formed within India but unfamiliar to a wider readership.
2. **Translation from Gujarati** — calques and structural patterns that don't transfer cleanly into English.
3. **Devotional register** — register-level pitfalls in translating religious / devotional prose.

## 3.15.1 What Goes On This Page (and What Doesn't)
Add a pitfall to this page when **all** of the following are true:

- It has been **fixed in at least three different BAPS manuscripts** by SAP editors. One-off corrections don't belong here.
- It is **not already covered** by a mainstream usage guide (Garner, Fowler, CMS, *New Hart's Rules*) — or, if it is, the SAP-specific guidance differs.
- The team **agrees** on the recommended form (no open variants).
- A **source** can be cited — an entry in OED, Garner, the *Hindu* style guide, or, where the rule is BAPS-internal, a clear note that the source is SAP editorial review.

If a candidate fails any of the above, raise it at the next editorial review meeting rather than adding it here.

## 3.15.2 Indian-English Patterns

### 3.15.2.1 *Do the Needful* / *Kindly Revert*
**The error:** Indian-English business formulas that read as stilted or unclear to a general BAPS readership outside India.

- ❌ *Kindly revert with your comments at the earliest.*
- ✅ *Please send your comments as soon as possible.*

- ❌ *Please do the needful and confirm.*
- ✅ *Please take the necessary action and confirm.*

**Why:** *Do the needful* and *kindly revert* are well-formed in Indian English but read as archaic or unclear to readers in the UK, US, and parts of the diaspora. Replace with direct, neutral phrasing.

**Source:** Both phrases carry a "chiefly Indian English" usage note in the [Oxford English Dictionary](https://www.oed.com/) (s.v. *needful*, *revert*). Garner's *Modern English Usage* (5th ed.) flags *revert* in the "reply" sense as "primarily an Indian-English usage; nonstandard elsewhere."

### 3.15.2.2 *Prepone*
**The error:** *Prepone* (= reschedule earlier) is well-established in Indian English but unrecognized outside India.

- ❌ *The meeting has been preponed to 9 a.m.*
- ✅ *The meeting has been moved up / brought forward / rescheduled earlier to 9 a.m.*

**Why:** Coined in Indian English as the antonym of *postpone*. Recognized as a legitimate regional coinage in the [OED](https://www.oed.com/) but flagged as "Indian." General readers will either misunderstand or notice it as a regionalism.

**Source:** OED entry for *prepone*, marked "Indian English."

### 3.15.2.3 *Only* and *Itself* for Emphasis
**The error:** Indian-English uses *only* and *itself* as discourse-level emphasizers in positions where general English would use intonation or rephrasing.

- ❌ *I told him that itself.* / *He came yesterday only.*
- ✅ *I told him exactly that.* / *He came only yesterday.* (= as recently as yesterday)
- ✅ *He came yesterday.* (if no emphasis is intended)

**Why:** The end-of-clause *only* and *itself* function in Indian English as focus markers (likely calqued from Hindi *hī* and Gujarati *ja*). In general English, *only* before the focused element is the standard placement, and *itself* in this emphatic sense is simply absent.

**Source:** Documented as a defining feature of Indian English in B. B. Kachru, *The Indianization of English* (Oxford, 1983); see also the Indian English usage notes in Garner's *Modern English Usage*.

### 3.15.2.4 *Cousin-Sister* / *Cousin-Brother*
**The error:** Indian English compounds *cousin* with the gendered sibling term to distinguish cousins from siblings.

- ❌ *His cousin-sister is studying in London.*
- ✅ *His cousin is studying in London.* (gender is rarely needed; if it is, recast: *his female cousin*, *his cousin Priya*)

**Why:** *Cousin-sister* / *cousin-brother* are Indian-English compounds reflecting a kinship distinction that English doesn't morphologically encode. In wider English usage, *cousin* is gender-neutral and the compound forms read as ungrammatical.

**Source:** Indian-English kinship terminology, documented in Mukherjee, *Indian English: Variation and Change* (Benjamins, 2010).

## 3.15.3 Translation-from-Gujarati Patterns

### 3.15.3.1 *The Doing of X* / Excessive Nominalization
**The error:** Gujarati often uses verbal nouns (*karavu*, *thavu*) where English would use a finite verb. Direct translation produces stilted nominal phrases.

- ❌ *The doing of bhakti is essential for the aspirant.*
- ✅ *The aspirant must do bhakti.* / *Bhakti is essential for the aspirant.*

- ❌ *Through the practising of meditation, peace is attained.*
- ✅ *Through meditation, one attains peace.* / *Meditation brings peace.*

**Why:** Gujarati's gerundive constructions are syntactically natural in Gujarati but produce awkward English. Editors should recast nominal phrases as verbal ones unless the abstraction is genuinely the topic (*"The practice of meditation has a long history in India"* — here *the practice of* is the subject).

**Source:** SAP editorial review; translation-team analysis of Gujarati→English calques.

### 3.15.3.2 *Going for Darshan / Arti*
**The error:** Indian-English uses *going for [event]* where general English uses *attending / going to [event]*.

- ❌ *We went for darshan at 6 a.m.*
- ✅ *We went to the mandir for darshan at 6 a.m.* / *We attended darshan at 6 a.m.*

**Why:** *Going for X* is a calque of the Gujarati *X-māṭe javu*. In general English, *for* + noun marks purpose only when the noun is unambiguous; for events, *to* (place) or *attend* (event) is the natural collocation.

**Source:** SAP editorial review; pattern recurrent in translation from Gujarati sources.

### 3.15.3.3 Overuse of *Very*, *Especially*, *Particularly*
**The error:** Gujarati emphasis particles (*khub*, *atishay*, *vishesh*) often translate into English as repeated intensifiers, which read as overwrought in devotional prose.

- ❌ *The devotee was very, very deeply moved by the discourse, especially by the part where Bhagwan Swaminarayan particularly explained the nature of bhakti.*
- ✅ *The devotee was deeply moved by the discourse — especially the part where Bhagwan Swaminarayan explained the nature of bhakti.*

**Why:** English intensifiers are weaker than their Gujarati counterparts; piling them up doesn't compound the emphasis, it dilutes it. One well-placed intensifier (or none) is usually stronger than a chain.

**Source:** Standard editorial guidance; cf. Strunk & White ("omit needless words"), Zinsser, *On Writing Well*. SAP-specific application to devotional prose.

## 3.15.4 Devotional-Register Pitfalls

### 3.15.4.1 Capitalizing Pronouns for Reverence (*He*, *His*)
**The error:** Translating Gujarati devotional texts, English drafts sometimes capitalize *He / His / Him* referring to Bhagwan Swaminarayan or the gurus — a convention common in older English devotional writing but increasingly avoided in modern usage.

- ❌ *Bhagwan Swaminarayan addressed the assembly. He explained the nature of dharma. His words moved the devotees.*
- ✅ *Bhagwan Swaminarayan addressed the assembly. He explained the nature of dharma. His words moved the devotees.* (lowercase *he*, *his*)

**Why:** Modern English style, including most contemporary religious publishing, uses lowercase pronouns for divine reference. Capitalization can read as old-fashioned or sectarian to a general readership. Reverence is conveyed by the choice of nouns (*Bhagwan*, *the Guru*, *the Satpurush*) and the surrounding register, not by pronoun case.

**Source:** *Chicago Manual of Style* §8.95 ("Religious names — pronouns referring to God and other deities are lowercased"). *New Hart's Rules* §5.2 takes the same line.

### 3.15.4.2 *Glory* / *Greatness* as Default Translations
**The error:** Gujarati *mahima* is reflexively translated as *glory* or *greatness*, even where a more precise English word would better fit the context.

- ❌ *He spoke of the glory of bhakti in every discourse.*
- ✅ Depending on context: *He spoke of the **value** of bhakti…* / *He spoke of the **importance** of bhakti…* / *He spoke of the **transformative power** of bhakti…*

**Why:** *Mahima* in Gujarati covers a semantic range — glory, greatness, importance, value, transformative power, significance — that English unpacks into distinct words. Picking the right English word for the context produces more precise prose than a default *glory*.

**Source:** SAP editorial review; translation-team analysis.

## 3.15.5 How to Propose a New Pitfall
Send a note via [Feedback](../feedback.md) with the subject *Pitfall: [short description]*. Include:

1. The error pattern (one sentence).
2. At least three examples from real manuscripts (anonymized if needed).
3. The recommended replacement.
4. A citable source (OED, Garner, Fowler, *Hindu* style guide, etc.) — or, if the rule is BAPS-internal, a note explaining why.

The maintainer reviews submissions monthly; accepted pitfalls go into the next page revision.

## 3.15.6 Reliable Sources
For checking whether a pattern is "Indian English" or general English, consult in this order:

1. **[Oxford English Dictionary](https://www.oed.com/) (OED)** — has explicit "Indian English" usage notes on the affected entries (*prepone*, *needful*, *revert*).
2. **Garner's *Modern English Usage*, 5th ed.** — has a substantial entry on Indian English and treats individual usages.
3. **Fowler's *Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, 4th ed. (Butterfield)** — BrE-focused; useful for register questions.
4. **The *Hindu* style guide** — codifies Indian English for an Indian newspaper audience; useful as a counterpoint when deciding what to keep vs. correct.
5. **Mukherjee, *Indian English: Variation and Change* (Benjamins, 2010)** — academic but indispensable for kinship terms, particle usage, and syntactic patterns.

For general English errors (not on this page), use Garner or Fowler directly.
