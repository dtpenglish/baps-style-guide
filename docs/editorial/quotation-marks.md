# 3.8 Quotation Marks
How to present an Indic term alongside its English translation.

For the general SAP rule (single quotes as the primary mark for highlighting; double for speech only), see [Punctuation §3.9.1](punctuation.md#391-quotation-marks).

## 3.8.1 The Four Cases
### 3.8.1.1 Simple Apposition — No Quotation Marks
When the Indic term and the translation are juxtaposed using **commas, the word *or*, dashes, parentheses, or brackets**, do **not** use quotation marks around the translation. The translation is in roman script.

- He immediately offered *dandvats* – prostrations.
- A bowl of *mamra*, or puffed rice, topped with peanuts.

### 3.8.1.2 Translation Clarified — Single Quotes around the Translation
With phrases such as *which means*, *translated as*, or *literally*, the translation is highlighting the gloss — set it in **single quotes** and roman:

> The Gujarati phrase is *Bhagwan bhaji leva*, meaning 'worship God'.

### 3.8.1.3 Quoted Speech — Double Quotes
When someone is actually speaking — dialogue, calling out, blessing — use double quotes for the speech, with single quotes for any nested gloss:

- He warned me: "*Jaldi*! Quickly!"
- We said, "*Kaka, kem chho*? – Uncle, how are you?"
- "*Bhagwan bhaji leva* – Worship God."
- "We call it *matru bhasha*," he explained, "the mother tongue."
- "*Agal*, *agal*," he called out. "Go forward, go forward." "*Dabi baju*, *dabi baju*. Go left, go left."
- They shouted, "*Hayso*!" meaning, 'let us pull together!'
- Pramukh Swami Maharaj blessed, "*Patthar satsang karavshe* – the stones will spread the glory of satsang."

In the last two examples, the *meaning* / *gloss* portion (set off by *meaning*, or implicit) takes single quotes — it's a highlight of the translation, not someone speaking the English version.

### 3.8.1.4 Mottoes and Slogans Presented as Phrases — Single Quotes
When a motto or slogan is being presented as a phrase (rather than spoken in dialogue), enclose it in **single quotes**. The Indic phrase is in italics; the translation is in roman.

- The BAPS motto '*Samp tya jamp* – Peace where there is unity' captures the spirit of unity.
- The unifying call '*Hayso*!' — 'let us pull together!' — punctuated the assembly.

If the motto is being **spoken** by someone in narrative ("They shouted, *Hayso!*"), apply the speech rule in 3.8.1.3 and use double quotes around the spoken text.

### 3.8.1.5 Explanations — No Quotation Marks
Explanations are presented **without** quotation marks:

- *rotlo*, an unleavened bread made from a variety of grains…
- An Indian drum called *dholak*…

## 3.8.2 Punctuation inside Italicized Words
> Do not italicize the punctuation marks in italicized words/phrases.

Set the punctuation in roman, even when the word or phrase around it is italic.

## 3.8.3 Quick Reference
| Construction | Marks | Translation form |
|---|---|---|
| Apposition with comma / *or* / dash / parens | None | Roman |
| *Which means* / *translated as* / *literally* | Single around the translation | Roman |
| Quoted speech (someone speaking) | Double around the speech; single around any nested gloss | Per case |
| Motto presented as a phrase | Single | Italic Indic + roman translation |
| Motto spoken aloud in narrative | Double (speech rule) | Italic Indic + roman translation |
| Plain explanation of a term | None | Roman |

## 3.8.4 Terms Used in a Special Sense
When a common English word is used in a particular, technical, or ironic sense for which the writer wants to flag the reader, use **single quotes on first use only**. In subsequent uses within the same publication, set the term in roman without quotation marks.

> The committee adopted what it called a 'soft launch' approach. The soft launch began on Magshar *sud* Padvo.

Reserve this device for words where the reader genuinely needs to be put on notice that an unusual sense is intended. Don't use it as a hedge.
